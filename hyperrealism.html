Hyperreality

Pushing the edge of what's real and what's not using detail, texture, color, and lighting.

The History of Hyperreality

Broken down into 3 stages, pre-digital imaging, during the rise of digital imaging, and current day.

Pre-Digital Imaging

Before digital editing softwares were widely available, hyperrealistic images were created in a different way. Using lighting, object placement, and other non-digital ways to edit a photo to achieve the hyperrealistic look. In this example, you can see that the car is bright and shiny, and is also in the foreground, center focus of the image.

During the Rise of Digital Imaging

During the rise of digital imaging, people started to push the boundary more of reality in iamges. Using editing softwares to place people or objects in unnatural places, changing the sizing of certain pieces of the image, or adjusting the color. This is where you see hyperrealistic edits truly come through in a more intense way.

Current Day

Today, hyperrealism is more present than ever before, while sometimes hiding under the surface with an intent to be discreet. Bright, perfect colors and reflectiions light up images, and make things feel so close you could touch them. Hyperreality in image is everywhere we go nowadays, on ads, on social media, everywhere we look there will be some sort of hyperrealistic image. Whether that's a makeup ad with a close-up of the product on someones face, or a colorgraded image made to push the boundaries of reality loudly, we are surrounded by hyperreality in image.

Aurelie Graillot

Graillot is a still life photographer who utilizes meticulous detail in her pieces, creating refined visual storytelling. She has an impeccable understanding of light, and uses it to transfomr the objects she photographs into incredible pieces of art. She focuses on bright colors, extreme detail, and seemingly perfect texture, creating something so lifelike it's almost too perfect.

Chen Wei

A Beijing-based artist, emerging since the mid 2000`s with his artistic voice. Chen is a master at creating, building intricate sets that almost resemble a diorama, and using LED signs and lights to create intense lighting. He uses incredible precision to compose each piece, often trying to encapsulate the feeling and environment of night clubs or night life. Constructing incredible hyperrealistic images with intense color and design, Chen is the perfect example of Hyperreality in image.

Karen Constine

Constine is a photographer who uses her art to explore themes of community, culture, and place. She plays with color, lighting, and texture to create hyperrealistic images with an emphasis on their visual dimensions. Her work has been exhibited in national and international curated exhibitions, and has also received numerous awards and publishings.

What Works

Hyperrealism can be very good for creating immersive images that catch the users attention. It allows photographers to show their editing skills and vision, resulting in incredible photos that become believable and realistic to viewers. Using bright colors and intense textures, photographers are able to create something so detailed that it's almost like it's right there in front of you. This works well for digital advertising, being able to create a makeup product that has extra shine and bright color, or a beach resort with empty spaces and crystal clear water. It's a perfect version of a less perfect reality, and people buy into it!

What Doesn't

While hyperreality can be very good for advertising and business, it can also do the oppsite. Seeing a seemingly perfect vacation spot, food, or other item in an ad, only to see it in real life and it be something totally different, is a part of hyperreality that can be dangerous. Imagine seeing an ad for a mcdonalds burger - you know it looks nothing like that, and it breaks the illusion of the hyperreality. Same with overly perfected skin in makeup ads, you know its too perfect to be real, so that perfection can't be attributed to the product. It can also cause visual overwhelm, with bright colors and intense textures distracting the user from the intent behind the image.
